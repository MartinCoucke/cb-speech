Philip N Jefferson: Navigating economic shocks - a monetary policymaker's perspective (2026-08-03)
Vice Chair Jefferson delivered an academic-style speech at Stanford analyzing how the Fed responds to supply and demand shocks, with a focus on two current challenges: a Middle East-driven energy price shock and the macroeconomic implications of AI. He noted that inflation remains above the 2% target while unemployment is near maximum employment, creating a dual-mandate tension. He indicated the FOMC held rates at 3.5–3.75% at its June meeting and flagged a conditional willingness to tighten further if inflation does not cool.
Jefferson's tone is modestly hawkish: he reaffirms a firm commitment to returning inflation to 2%, explicitly raises the possibility of reconsidering the current policy stance if inflation does not soon decline, and highlights risks of inflation becoming entrenched amid overlapping shocks. However, he stops short of signaling imminent hikes and acknowledges balanced risks to both sides of the mandate.
  • “in a scenario where actual inflation does not start to cool down soon, I believe that it could be appropriate to reconsider our current policy stance to ensure we fulfill our commitment to deliver price stability”

John C Williams — Federal Reserve
John C Williams: Stability of Thy Times (2026-08-03)
NY Fed President John Williams describes a resilient U.S. economy with solid GDP growth near 2% and a stable labor market, but notes inflation running at approximately 4%, well above the 2% target. He attributes elevated inflation to tariffs, Middle East supply disruptions, and AI-driven demand surges, while expressing confidence that inflation has peaked and will gradually decline toward 2% by 2028. Williams indicates the current fed funds rate of 3.5–3.75% is 'well positioned' and signals no imminent policy changes.
Williams strikes a modestly hawkish tone by emphasizing that inflation is 'unquestionably too high' and that restoring price stability is 'imperative,' while defending the current restrictive stance as appropriate. However, his expectation that inflation has peaked and will gradually decline, combined with a hold decision and no explicit tightening bias, tempers the hawkishness.
  • “Inflation is unquestionably too high at about 4 percent, well above the FOMC's longer-run goal of 2 percent.”
  • “With inflation running high, it is imperative that we restore it to the Federal Reserve's 2 percent longer-run goal on a sustained basis. The current stance of monetary policy is well positioned to do that.”
